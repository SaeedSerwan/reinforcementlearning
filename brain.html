The brain, and what still does not work.

An algorithm built for engineering reasons turned out to describe a signal in the brain. And a lecture can make reinforcement learning look more finished than it is: here are its honest limits, and two directions people are working on.

01Dopamine and a prediction that failed

Dopamine neurons in the midbrain were long thought to signal reward. In the 1980s and 90s Wolfram Schultz recorded them in monkeys: a light comes on, and a little later a drop of juice arrives.

SituationAt the light (cue)At the juice
Before learning: juice arrives unexpectedlyno responseburst of firing
After learning: light, then juiceburst of firingno response
After learning, but the juice is withheldburst of firingfiring drops below normal

"Dopamine = reward" explains only the first row. In 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague gave the answer: the neurons behave like the TD error, a reward prediction error. Better or worse than expected.

02The TD error behaves the same way

Simulate the experiment with the plain TD(0) of Part 4. Each trial has 20 time steps; before the first cue nothing can be predicted. Move through the trials.

A TD learner in the monkey experiment

α = 0.2, γ = 1.

With two cues (an instruction cue that says which lever, then a trigger cue that says when), the surprise moves back past the trigger to the instruction cue, as Schultz's recordings did. It stops at the earliest event that reliably predicts the juice: before that, nothing does.

Key idea: the TD error and dopamine both move from the reward to the earliest reliable predictor of it.

03Actor and critic in the brain?

A second, less settled link. Deep in the brain, the striatum is involved in action planning, decision making, motivation and reward. Two of its parts have been proposed to play the roles of actor and critic: the dorsolateral striatum in choosing actions, the ventral striatum in prediction. And dopamine, the TD error, reaches both, just as δ teaches both actor and critic.

After Takahashi, Schoenbaum & Niv (2008), "Silencing the critics". Good evidence, but treat it as a hypothesis.

04Limit 1: results depend on luck

The same program with different random numbers can succeed or fail. In supervised learning the data are fixed; an RL agent creates its own data. A lucky early success gives good data, which gives a better policy, which gives better data; an unlucky start does the opposite.

Ten runs of the same Q-learner

Q-learning on the pendulum boxes of Part 6, identical settings, at most 400 trials. Only the random seed differs.

So: never judge an RL method by one run. Every chart on this site that matters is an average of many runs; see also the pendulum comparison.

05Limit 2: sample inefficiency

TaskExperience the learner neededA person
Small maze (Q-learning)about 200 episodessolves it by looking at it once
Inverted pendulum (actor–critic)about 100 fallsa few tries
Atari games (one of the best agents of 2017)about 18 million frames: 83 hours of playa few minutes

In a simulator, experience costs only computer time. On a real robot, every trial costs time, wear and sometimes damage. Why are people so much faster? We know how the world works before we start, and we are often shown what to do. Those are the two directions below.

Key idea: model-free learning needs a lot of experience, because each step teaches it only a little.

using a model

Plan ahead

Try possible futures in your head before acting. Value iteration on the maze (Part 4) is the simplest case.

The difficulty: a learned model is never exactly right, and a policy trained inside it will find and exploit its mistakes.

Key idea: a learned model lets the agent practise in imagination, which saves real experience.

07Direction 2: learning from demonstrations

You would not expect a three-year-old to learn to tie shoelaces by trial and error. Imitation learning learns from an expert's trajectories instead of a reward. The simplest form, behavioural cloning, copies the expert's action in every state it has seen. Its weakness: one small mistake leads to a state the expert never visited, with no example for it, and the errors compound.

Cloning an expert in the big maze

Blue squares: states where the clone has an expert example. Click a square to start the clone there.

DAgger fixes the gaps: let the learner act, ask the expert what they would have done in the states the learner reached, add those examples, retrain, repeat. More robust, but tedious for the human.

why bother

Rewards are hard to write

Reward a racing agent only for crossing the finish line and it may drive back and forth across the line. And how would you write a reward for "drive safely and politely"?

inverse RL

Learn the reward instead

Watch the expert and infer what goal they must be pursuing; then run ordinary RL on it. One method learned several games from 20–30 demonstrations.

in practice

Combine both

A robot learned to catch a ball in a cup: first by imitating a human, then improving by trial and error, about 100 tries.

Key idea: demonstrations give the agent a good starting policy instead of a random one.

08The whole lecture in eight lines

1 · Agent and environmentthe agent is what it directly controls; the rest is environment
2 · MDPstates, actions, rewards; a policy; the return; find the policy with the highest return
3 · Monte Carlosample episodes, average returns into a Q-table, act ε-greedily
4 · Temporal differencecompare each step with the estimate of the next: SARSA, Expected SARSA, Q-learning
5 · Policy gradient, actor–criticchange action probabilities in proportion to how good the action was
6 · Inverted pendulumboxes; the signed eligibility remembers the direction; the TD error says whether to reverse it

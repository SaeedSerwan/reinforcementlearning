A small maze, and the simplest way to learn.

On a grid, every state is one square, so everything can be drawn. This tiny maze is enough to meet the big ideas: world models, sampling, returns, value functions, exploration versus exploitation, and the first learning algorithm, Monte Carlo.

01The task

Six columns, four rows and an L-shaped wall. The agent starts in the bottom-left corner and must reach the goal in the top-right corner. It can move up, down, left or right; a move into a wall or off the grid leaves it where it is. An episode ends at the goal or after 25 steps. Notice the dead-end pocket next to the start: walking right first is a trap.

MDP partIn this maze
StateTwo numbers: the coordinates (column, row)
ActionOne number 0–3: up, down, left, right
Reward0 for entering the goal square, −1 for every other step
EpisodeEnds at the goal or after 25 steps

Why −1 per step? Because then the total reward is minus the number of steps used: maximising it means getting there as fast as possible. The shortest route is 8 moves (up the left edge, then along the top), so the best total is −7. Other choices of reward teach very different behaviour: see the reward lab.

Key idea: the reward does not tell the agent how to solve the task, only how well it did. Whatever we reward is what it will learn.

02World models: what we see, what the agent sees

We solve the maze at a glance because we understand it: the numbers are coordinates, 3 means "right", walls block. An agent that knows this has a world model: the function \(p(s', r \mid s, a)\) that says what happens after each action. The methods on this page are model-free: the agent does not have that function. States and actions are meaningless numbers to it.

Ask the world model

Click a square, choose an action. We can answer; the agent has to find out by trying.

Key idea: without a world model, the only way to learn what an action does is to try it.

03Starting from nothing: random actions

With no information, the agent can only choose at random: every action has probability ¼ in every state. Most episodes go nowhere. But the agent writes down everything that happens. Trying actions is called sampling; the recorded sequences are trajectories. Now and then, by luck, one reaches the goal, and those are the valuable ones.

Key idea: random actions are a valid start: they produce the experience that learning needs.

Key idea: compute returns backwards: each step only needs its own reward and the return of the step after it.

05From experience to a better policy

How do we use the returns? The direct idea: push up the probability of actions followed by good returns, push down those followed by bad ones (a policy gradient, Part 5). But "good" needs a reference point: is −3 good? Only compared with what we expected. That expected return is a value function:

\[ v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s\right] \qquad q_\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s,\ A_t = a\right] \]

06A value is an average

A value function stores the average return, and nothing else. Two actions can have the same value and very different risks.

Same value, different risk

A: the long safe way, always about −4. B: a door that is open half the time: −1.5 or −6.5.

Action B

Research areas such as distributional RL (keep the whole histogram) and Bayesian RL (keep the uncertainty of each estimate) try to keep this lost information.

Key idea: a value is an average. It tells you how good an action is on average, not how risky it is.

07Evaluation by averaging: the Q-table

The simplest way to estimate Q: keep one entry per square and action (18 open squares × 4 actions = 72 numbers, all zero at the start) and, for every new return, move the entry a small step towards it:

\[ Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\,\big(G_t - Q(S_t, A_t)\big) \]

Each square is drawn as four triangles, one per action. Darker red = more negative. Apply the lucky episode, one entry at a time.

Update the table

Working backwards through the lucky episode, α = 0.1. Red = negative, white = 0.

Something uncomfortable: every entry we touched became negative, while untried actions are still 0. After this good episode, the actions that led to the goal look worse than the untried ones. It sorts itself out as untried actions get tried and get their own, more negative, values.

Evaluation and improvement feed each other

Our policy picks the action with the highest Q. A better Q gives a better policy; a new policy needs a new evaluation. Round and round: generalised policy iteration. It settles when Q = q*, and then the greedy policy is optimal.

Key idea: a Q-table is a list of running averages, one per square and action, nudged towards each new return.

08Exploration versus exploitation

If the agent always picks the highest-rated action, it never tries anything new, and whatever is wrong in the table stays wrong. It needs a balance: explore to improve, exploit to do well. The simple trick is ε-greedy: with probability ε choose a random action, otherwise the best one. Start with ε = 0.9 (the table is still wrong), end with ε = 0.1.

Same knowledge, three exploration rates

All three agents already have the correct table. Only ε differs.

Check

With ε = 0.2 and four actions, the best action is chosen with probability 0.8 + 0.2/4 = 0.85, not 0.8: the random choice can pick it too.

Key idea: ε-greedy mixes the two needs: mostly use what you know, sometimes try something else.

09Too little exploration: settling for less

Why not simply set ε = 0 and always take the best action? Because "best" only means best among what the agent has tried. If it stops exploring too early, it locks onto the first thing that worked and never finds out that something far better exists.

A new maze shows it. There are now two goals: a small one (+1) just two steps from the start, and a big one (+10) at the far end. Every step costs 0 and γ = 0.9, so the big reward is clearly better: worth about \(10 \times 0.9^5 \approx 5.9\) from the start, against \(1 \times 0.9 = 0.9\) for the small one. The learner is Q-learning; only the exploration schedule changes.

The agents with too little exploration did not fail to learn: they learned perfectly well how to reach the small reward. They never saw the big one, so as far as they know, the small reward is the best there is.
The lesson

A greedy agent can only exploit what it has already discovered. Early on its estimates are poor, so it must explore a lot; only once it has seen enough of the world can it safely stop. Explore too little, or stop too soon, and it converges to a deterministic policy that is not optimal, and is completely confident about it.

Why did the first maze not have this problem?

There every step cost −1, so a Q-value of 0 for an untried action looked better than any tried one: the zeros were optimistic and pushed the agent to try everything. Here the first reward found is positive, so the zeros are pessimistic: once the small reward is found, nothing else looks attractive.

Key idea: too little exploration does not fail loudly. The agent becomes confident in a policy that is merely good enough.

10Monte Carlo control

Monte Carlo is the general name for methods that use repeated random sampling. Here: generate a whole episode with ε-greedy, then go backwards through it, moving every visited entry towards its return.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{for } t = T{-}1, \dots, 1, 0: \\ &\qquad G \leftarrow R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G \\ &\qquad Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\,\big(G - Q(S_t,A_t)\big) \end{aligned} \]

Train it

300 episodes, α = 0.1, γ = 0.8, ε from 0.9 down to 0.1. Click a square to add or remove a wall and start again.

Total reward per episode (grey) and the average of the last 10 (red)

Random sampling, averaging, and mostly choosing what looked best: enough to solve the maze from meaningless numbers. Not perfectly, and slowly: Part 4 shows why.

Key idea: Monte Carlo control = play a whole episode, then move every visited entry towards its return.

11A problem: who deserves the credit?

Monte Carlo judges whole episodes. In a good episode every action is rated up, including the wasted moves; in a bad episode every action is rated down, including the good ones. It can only hope that many episodes average this out.

Which action in a long sequence deserves the credit or the blame? This is the credit assignment problem, and it is where temporal difference starts.

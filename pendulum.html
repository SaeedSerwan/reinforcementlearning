The inverted pendulum: which push was wrong?

A pole on a cart, a controller that can only push left or right, and a single number when the pole has already fallen. This has been the standard first test for learning controllers for almost sixty years. Try it yourself first, then watch a learner from 1983 work out which of its pushes caused the fall and which way it should have pushed instead.

01The problem: try it yourself

A pole is hinged to a cart on a short track. Keep the pole within 12° of vertical and the cart within ±2.4 m. Left alone, the pole falls in about a second. Hold ← or → (or the buttons) to push the cart. Slow motion helps.

02The physical system

Four numbers describe the system completely: the cart position \(x\), its velocity \(\dot x\), the pole angle \(\theta\) (0 = upright, positive = leaning right) and the angular velocity \(\dot\theta\). The angle alone is not Markov: a pole at 3° that is falling needs a different push from one at 3° that is coming back. The action is a force of ±10 N, 50 times per second.

\[ \ddot\theta = \frac{g\sin\theta - \cos\theta\,\dfrac{F + m l \dot\theta^2 \sin\theta}{M+m}}{l\left(\dfrac43 - \dfrac{m\cos^2\theta}{M+m}\right)}, \qquad \ddot x = \frac{F + m l\big(\dot\theta^2\sin\theta - \ddot\theta\cos\theta\big)}{M+m} \]

g = 9.8 m/s², cart M = 1 kg, pole m = 0.1 kg, half-length l = 0.5 m, F = ±10 N, time step 0.02 s (Barto, Sutton & Anderson 1983; Gymnasium's CartPole).

These equations are the world model. A control engineer would start from them. The learner is not given them: it does not know there is gravity, a hinge, or what the four numbers mean. We take the model-free route because for many real systems nobody can write such equations down.

03The pendulum as an MDP

States\((x, \dot x, \theta, \dot\theta)\): continuous, so infinitely many
Actionspush left, push right
Failure (end)\(|\theta| > 12°\) or \(|x| > 2.4\) m

Two reward conventions lead to the same behaviour. Gymnasium gives +1 for every step the pole is up. Barto, Sutton and Anderson give 0 at every step and −1 at failure. We use the second: the learner's only information is a single −1, when the pole has already fallen.

04Why is this hard for a learner?

1

Continuous states

A table needs finitely many rows; the pole never visits exactly the same state twice.

2

No teacher

Nobody says "you should have pushed left". No labelled examples.

3

Late, unspecific feedback

The pole falls at step 23. Which of the 23 pushes was wrong? And was the mistake pushing left or pushing right? The −1 does not say.

Barto, Sutton and Anderson called this the credit assignment problem. The rest of this page is how they solved it.

05A first idea: write the rule by hand

Before any learning: can we just write a rule? The obvious one, "push towards the side the pole leans", overcorrects: it keeps pushing until the pole has passed the vertical, by which time the pole is rotating fast the other way. Adding the angular velocity, reacting to where the pole is going, changes everything.

Hand-written rules

All start with the pole at 2°, at rest. The trial stops at 1,000 steps (20 s).

The velocity matters as much as the position, and finding that (and the 0.5) took insight into the physics. Can a program discover such a rule by itself, from the failure signal alone?

06Step 1: make the states countable

The oldest solution (Michie & Chambers, 1968): cut each variable into a few intervals. 3 for the cart position, 3 for its velocity, 6 for the angle (finer near vertical) and 3 for the angular velocity. Every combination is one box: 3 × 3 × 6 × 3 = 162 boxes. The learner is told only the number of the current box. The price: all states inside a box are treated as identical.

The decoder at work

07Step 2: Q-learning on the boxes

No teacher? We know the answer from Part 4: Q-learning with a table of 162 × 2 entries, all zero, reward −1 at failure. No ε is needed: untried actions (0) always look better than tried ones (negative), so the agent explores by itself: optimistic initialisation. A run counts as solved when the pole stays up for 20,000 steps (400 s).

Q-learning learns to balance

α = 0.5, γ = 0.99, each trial starts from a slightly random state. Each dot is one trial (log scale).

It works, with no knowledge of physics. But it usually needs hundreds of falls. Why? In Q-learning, blame travels back one box at a time, and only after both actions in the later box have failed. If the real mistake was fifteen steps before the fall, the news needs a long chain of failures to get there.

08Idea 1: a memory with a sign

Barto, Sutton and Anderson give each box two numbers. The weight \(w\) is long-term memory, the box's preference: positive pushes right, negative pushes left. The eligibility \(e\) is short-term memory: what was done in this box recently, and in which direction. Visiting a box and pushing right adds to its e; pushing left subtracts; every step all e shrink by λ = 0.9.

\[ e_i \leftarrow \lambda\, e_i + (1-\lambda)\big(y - \tfrac12\big)\, x_i \]

y = 1 for a push to the right, 0 for a push to the left; \(x_i\) = 1 for the current box, 0 for all others. After 20 steps without a visit about 12% of a box's eligibility is left.

Idea 2: reinforcement × eligibility gives the direction

When a reinforcement \(\hat r\) arrives, every box changes its weight by \(\alpha\,\hat r\, e_i\). A product of two signs. Click a case:

\[ w_i \leftarrow w_i + \alpha\, \hat r\, e_i \qquad (\alpha = 1000) \]

The first failure, step by step

Trial 1 of a run: random pushes. Bars: the signed eligibility of each box visited. At the fall, the change Δw = α · (−1) · e.

One undirected number, \(\hat r = -1\), produces a correctly directed correction in every box, because each box remembers its own direction. Blame makes you do the opposite, praise makes you do the same, in proportion to how recently you acted. The rule is not a judge: some blame lands on innocent pushes, but over many failures wrongly placed blame points in changing directions and cancels out, while deserved blame keeps pointing the same way.

09Idea 3: a critic that predicts trouble

With the raw failure signal, learning happens only at the instant of a fall, when the guilty push may be far back and its eligibility faded. So add a second element that learns to predict failure: the critic. For each box it learns \(p\) between −1 (failure is near) and 0 (safe), by TD(0), and turns the rare \(r\) into an internal signal at every step:

\[ \hat r = r + \gamma\, p(\text{new box}) - p(\text{old box}) \qquad (\gamma = 0.95) \]

You have seen this formula: it is the TD error of Part 4. Moving from a safe box into a dangerous one: \(\hat r < 0\), blame now. Escaping back to safety: \(\hat r > 0\), praise.

The critic warns before the pole falls

A trial after some learning: the last 60 steps before a fall.

Instead of 0, 0, 0, …, −1 the actor now gets a running commentary: worse… better… worse. The blame arrives earlier, while the push that caused the trouble is still eligible.

10The complete system: train it

The 1983 architecture: a decoder (four numbers → box), an actor (ASE: weights and signed eligibilities, chooses the push) and a critic (ACE: predictions, sends \(\hat r\)). It is the actor–critic of Part 5 (the eligibility is "the direction that makes the taken action more likely", \(\hat r\) is "how good it was"), discovered before the name existed.

Algorithm 5: ASE/ACE, live

α = 1000, β = 0.5, γ = 0.95, λ = 0.9, λᵥ = 0.8. Solved = 20,000 steps without failing.

The trained controller is not elegant: the pole wobbles and the cart wanders. It was never asked to be elegant; the reward only says "do not fail". You get what you reward.

12Does the critic matter?

One run proves little. Run each learner with ten different random seeds, at most 400 trials each.

Two reasons the actor–critic wins: the traces give credit to many recent boxes at once, and the critic gives a learning signal at every step instead of one per failure. And the honest part: results depend on the seed. Never trust a single run.

13Lessons from the inverted pendulum

1

The pipeline

States, actions, reward → make the states manageable → choose an algorithm → run it many times.

2

Credit assignment

When was the mistake: traces, and a critic that moves the signal earlier. Which direction: the sign of the trace times the sign of the TD error.

3

The reward defines the result

We rewarded "do not fail" and got a wobbly controller that does not fail.

4

The limits of tables

4 variables gave 162 boxes; 10 variables with 6 intervals each would need 60 million. The boxes were designed by hand.

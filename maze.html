A small maze, and the simplest way to learn.

On a grid, every state is one square, so everything can be drawn. This tiny maze is enough to meet the big ideas: world models, sampling, returns, value functions, exploration versus exploitation, and the first learning algorithm, Monte Carlo.

01The task

Five columns, three rows and one wall. The rat starts bottom left and must reach the cheese bottom right. It can move up, down, left or right; a move into the wall or off the grid leaves it where it is. An episode ends at the cheese or after 20 steps.

MDP partIn this maze
StateTwo numbers: the coordinates (column, row)
ActionOne number 0–3: up, down, left, right
Reward0 for entering the cheese square, −1 for every other step
EpisodeEnds at the cheese or after 20 steps

Why −1 per step? Because then the total reward is minus the number of steps used. Maximising it means: get there as fast as possible. The shortest route is 8 moves: total −7.

02World models: what we see, what the agent sees

We solve the maze at a glance because we understand it: the numbers are coordinates, 3 means "right", walls block. An agent that knows this has a world model: the function \(p(s', r \mid s, a)\) that says what happens after each action. The methods on this page are model-free: the agent does not have that function. States and actions are meaningless numbers to it.

Ask the world model

Click a square, choose an action. We can answer; the agent has to find out by trying.

03Starting from nothing: random actions

With no information, the agent can only choose at random: every action has probability ¼ in every state. Most episodes go nowhere. But the agent writes down everything that happens. Trying actions is called sampling; the recorded sequences are trajectories. Now and then, by luck, one reaches the cheese, and those are the valuable ones.

05From experience to a better policy

How do we use the returns? The direct idea: push up the probability of actions followed by good returns, push down those followed by bad ones (a policy gradient, Part 5). But "good" needs a reference point: is −2.4 good? Only compared with what we expected. That expected return is a value function:

\[ v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s\right] \qquad q_\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s,\ A_t = a\right] \]
V(s)
state value: the return to expect from square s, then following the policy π
Q(s, a)
action value: the return to expect after taking action a in square s, then following π
v*, q*
the same for the best possible policy

06A value is an average

A value function stores the average return, and nothing else. Two actions can have the same value and very different risks.

07Evaluation by averaging: the Q-table

The simplest way to estimate Q: keep one entry per square and action (12 squares × 4 actions = 48 numbers, all zero at the start) and, for every new return, move the entry a small step towards it:

\[ Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\,\big(G_t - Q(S_t, A_t)\big) \]

Each square is drawn as four triangles, one per action. Darker red = more negative. Apply the lucky episode, one entry at a time.

Update the table

Working backwards through the lucky episode, α = 0.1.

Something uncomfortable: every entry we touched became negative, while untried actions are still 0. After this good episode, the actions that led to the cheese look worse than the untried ones. It sorts itself out as untried actions get tried and get their own, more negative, values.

Evaluation and improvement feed each other

Our policy picks the action with the highest Q. A better Q gives a better policy; a new policy needs a new evaluation. Round and round: generalised policy iteration. It settles when Q = q*, and then the greedy policy is optimal.

08Exploration versus exploitation

If the agent always picks the highest-rated action, it never tries anything new, and whatever is wrong in the table stays wrong. It needs a balance: explore to improve, exploit to do well. The simple trick is ε-greedy: with probability ε choose a random action, otherwise the best one. Start with ε = 0.9 (the table is still wrong), end with ε = 0.1.

Same knowledge, three exploration rates

All three rats already have the correct table. Only ε differs.

Check

With ε = 0.2 and four actions, the best action is chosen with probability 0.8 + 0.2/4 = 0.85, not 0.8: the random choice can pick it too.

09Too little exploration: settling for less

Why not simply set ε = 0 and always take the best action? Because "best" only means best among what the agent has tried. If it stops exploring too early, it locks onto the first thing that worked and never finds out that something far better exists.

A new maze shows it. There are now two cheeses: a small one (+1) just two steps from the start, and a big one (+10) at the far end. Every step costs 0 and γ = 0.9, so the big cheese is clearly better: worth about \(10 \times 0.9^5 \approx 5.9\) from the start, against \(1 \times 0.9 = 0.9\) for the small one. The learner is Q-learning; only the exploration schedule changes.

One run is luck. Fifty runs are evidence.

Each schedule is trained 50 times (300 episodes each). How often does the final greedy policy go to the big cheese?

The agents with too little exploration did not fail to learn: they learned perfectly well how to reach the small cheese. They never saw the big one, so as far as they know, the small cheese is the best there is.
The lesson

A greedy agent can only exploit what it has already discovered. Early on its estimates are poor, so it must explore a lot; only once it has seen enough of the world can it safely stop. Explore too little, or stop too soon, and it converges to a deterministic policy that is not optimal, and is completely confident about it.

Why did the first maze not have this problem?

There every step cost −1, so a Q-value of 0 for an untried action looked better than any tried one: the zeros were optimistic and pushed the agent to try everything. Here the first reward found is positive, so the zeros are pessimistic: once the small cheese is found, nothing else looks attractive.

10Algorithm 1: Monte Carlo control

Monte Carlo is the general name for methods that use repeated random sampling. Here: generate a whole episode with ε-greedy, then go backwards through it, moving every visited entry towards its return.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{for } t = T{-}1, \dots, 1, 0: \\ &\qquad G \leftarrow R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G \\ &\qquad Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\,\big(G - Q(S_t,A_t)\big) \end{aligned} \]

Train it

300 episodes, α = 0.1, γ = 0.8, ε from 0.9 down to 0.1. Click squares to add or remove walls.

Total reward per episode (grey) and the average of the last 10 (red)

Random sampling, averaging, and mostly choosing what looked best: enough to solve the maze from meaningless numbers. Not perfectly, and slowly: Part 4 shows why.

11A problem: who deserves the credit?

Monte Carlo judges whole episodes. In a good episode every action is rated up, including the wasted moves; in a bad episode every action is rated down, including the good ones. It can only hope that many episodes average this out.

The entry Q((4,3), left) is −0.5. Step 0 moved away from the cheese. What is the new value after this episode?

Which action in a long sequence deserves the credit or the blame? This is the credit assignment problem, and it is where temporal difference starts.
